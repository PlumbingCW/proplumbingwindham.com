How to Plumb a Bathroom Sink: What Windham, CT Homes Need to Know

A bathroom sink has three jobs for the pipes: bring water in, take wastewater out and let the drain breathe. Here is what each part does and where a project can go wrong.

Written by the Windham Pro Plumbing team. Published November 14, 2026.

Overview.

The three systems behind a sink

A sink connects to three systems. Supply lines bring hot and cold water to the faucet. The drain carries used water away through a trap. The vent lets air in so the drain flows freely and keeps sewer gas out.

People often focus on the faucet and forget the vent. A drain that gurgles or runs slowly after a new install often has a venting issue rather than a clog.

Water in.

Supply lines and shutoffs

Each faucet gets a hot and a cold line from the wall or floor. Between the wall pipe and the faucet sits a shutoff valve, called an angle stop, which lets you turn off the water to the sink without shutting the whole house down.

Flexible braided lines connect the valve to the faucet. They are easy to install but should be replaced if they look worn. A fitting hand-tightened and then a quarter turn with a wrench is usually enough.

Windham note.

What Connecticut code means for a bathroom sink

The 2022 Connecticut State Building Code, effective October 1, 2022, adopts the 2021 International Plumbing Code (IPC). That code sets rules for drain size, venting and trap design, and a plumbing permit may be required for new work depending on the town.

Check with your local building office before starting. Our plumbing installation service can tell you what applies at your address and handle the work to code.

Water out.

The drain, the P-trap and the vent

Below the sink basin, the tailpiece drops into a P-trap, a curved section that holds a small amount of water. That water seals the pipe against sewer gas. From the trap, the drain runs to the wall and joins the drain stack, which is vented to the roof.

The slope matters. A drain that runs too flat will clog, and one that is too steep can siphon the trap dry. Code sets limits on the distance between the trap and the vent, which is where many DIY layouts go wrong.

Steps.

Steps in the order they happen

A new sink in an existing bathroom is a modest job. In a remodel or new bathroom, the order matters even more.

  • Shut off the water at the main or the stops and drain the lines.
  • Set the faucet on the sink or counter before the sink goes in place.
  • Install the drain body and the pop-up, using putty or gaskets as the part requires.
  • Set the sink and secure it to the counter or wall as the type requires.
  • Connect the supply lines to the stops and the faucet.
  • Assemble the trap and connect it to the wall drain.
  • Turn the water on slowly, check every joint and run water for several minutes.
Pitfalls.

Where DIY sink projects go wrong

The common mistakes are overtightened plastic nuts, a P-trap that does not line up with the wall drain and a missing vent. Another is cutting into a wall without knowing where lines run. In older houses, galvanized and copper lines can be fragile and break when you move them.

If the drain in the wall is old cast iron or galvanized, connecting to it takes the right fittings and care. Our repiping page covers what we do when old supply lines are in the way.

Preparation.

Parts to gather before you start

A tidy parts list saves multiple trips. For a typical vanity sink you need a faucet, two braided supply lines, a pop-up drain assembly, a P-trap kit, plumber's putty or a gasket, thread tape and a few hand tools. An adjustable wrench, a basin wrench and a bucket cover most of the work.

A basin wrench is worth having because the nuts that hold a faucet in place sit in a cramped spot behind the sink. Check that the supply lines are long enough to reach the stops without a sharp bend.

Testing.

Checking your work

After the sink is connected, run the water for several minutes and look at every joint with a dry paper towel. A damp towel shows a slow drip that your eyes miss. Fill the basin and let it drain, because a fast release puts more stress on the trap joints than a trickle does.

Check again after an hour and the next morning. Small weeps often show up only after the parts have settled.

Next step.

When to hire a plumber

A swap of a faucet or a sink in the same place is a reasonable DIY job. A new sink in a new location, a new vent or work inside the wall is better left to a plumber, particularly if a permit is needed.

Common Questions.

Windham bathroom sink plumbing questions

Do I need a permit to replace a bathroom sink in Windham?

A like-for-like swap often does not, but new locations and new lines may. Check with the building office for your address or ask us.

Why does my new sink gurgle?

A gurgle usually means a venting problem or a trap that is too far from the vent.

Can I move a sink to the other wall?

Often yes, but the drain needs slope and a vent. A plumber should review the layout.

What size is a bathroom sink drain?

Many bathroom sinks use a smaller drain than kitchen sinks. Code and the fixture set the size.

Should I use plumber putty or silicone?

Follow the manufacturer. Some drains need putty, others need a gasket, and some stone sinks need silicone.
