Overview.

Installing a water heater is planning work, not a scramble

A water heater install you schedule is the cheapest way to get one. You pick the unit, we confirm the space and the venting, and the work happens on a day that suits you instead of in the middle of a failure.

This page covers planned installs in new homes, remodels, finished basements and upgrades. If your tank is already leaking, our replacement service handles the urgent version.

Scope.

Three jobs this service covers

Most installations fall into one of three groups, and each has different prep.

New construction

We rough in supply and drain lines, set the venting and finish the heater once the framing and inspections are ready.

Remodel or relocation

Moving a heater to a new closet or utility room means new piping, venting and sometimes a new gas or electrical run.

Upgrade

Going from a small tank to a larger one, adding a drain pan or fitting a code-compliant relief valve line before it becomes a problem.

Site check.

Space, venting and clearance come first

Before we order anything we look at where the heater will sit. A gas unit needs combustion air and a vent path that rises and terminates correctly. An electric unit needs clearance for service and a dedicated circuit of the right size.

We also check the floor. A heater on a finished floor needs a pan with a drain line so a future leak goes somewhere useful. A basement with a floor drain is easier than a second-floor closet.

Options.

Tank, hybrid or tankless: a short comparison

Most homes in the area still use a standard tank, but newer builds and remodels sometimes make other options worth a look. The comparison below is the one we walk through on the phone.

Standard tank

  • Lowest purchase price.
  • Simple to repair and replace.
  • Uses standby energy all day.
  • Takes up floor space.

Tankless

  • Endless hot water at a set flow rate.
  • Smaller footprint on the wall.
  • Needs larger gas or electric supply.
  • Yearly flush recommended.

Our page on tankless water heaters covers sizing and flushing in more detail, and we can talk through the choice on the call.

Code.

Code and permits for a new install

Connecticut adopts the International Plumbing Code (IPC) through its State Building Code, which sets requirements for relief valves, discharge piping, shutoff valves and venting. Many towns also ask for a permit when a heater is added or moved.

We ask for your street address when we book so we can tell you what applies in Windham, Mansfield, Coventry, Columbia or Lebanon before work starts. Skipping that step is how a good install ends up failing an inspection later.

Common Questions.

Questions about installing a water heater

How far ahead should I book a water heater installation?

As soon as you know the date. A scheduled install lets us order the right unit and check venting first. For a failed tank, call the 24/7 line instead.

Can you move my water heater to another room?

Often yes. It needs new supply, venting and drain lines and sometimes a gas or electrical run. We look at the old and new locations before quoting.

Do you install heaters in new construction?

Yes. We work with builders and homeowners during framing and finish, and we coordinate with inspections where the town requires them.

Do I need an expansion tank?

Many closed plumbing systems benefit from one, and some local rules ask for it. We check your system pressure and tell you whether one is needed.

Should I install a tankless unit instead of a tank?

It depends on your gas or electric capacity, household size and budget. We can walk you through the trade-offs on the call.

Do you haul away the old unit?

Yes, as part of the job. We drain, disconnect and remove it.
